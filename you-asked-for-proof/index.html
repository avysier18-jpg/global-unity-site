Act 1 — Retirement Risk Shifted to Workers

Before, many long-tenured workers—especially at large employers and unionized workplaces—had pensions that offered more predictable retirement income than today’s system. It was not universal, and it was not perfectly guaranteed, but the big difference was this: if the plan came up short, the company carried most of the shortfall risk, not the worker. Over time, a mix of policy changes and business incentives reshaped retirement. ERISA (1974) created protections for retirement plans and introduced IRAs. Later tax-code changes—including the Revenue Act of 1978 and 1981 IRS rules—accelerated 401(k)-style salary deferrals. Employers steadily shifted away from pensions toward market-linked retirement accounts, moving more retirement risk onto workers. Pensions were market-exposed too—but the sponsor carried the funding and shortfall risk. 401(k)s move that residual risk onto workers. This shift increased portability and reduced employer liability, but it also tied retirement security more tightly to financial markets. If markets crash, workers can lose years of savings—even if they did nothing wrong. “Over time, employers shifted from pensions to market-linked accounts, moving retirement risk onto workers.”
