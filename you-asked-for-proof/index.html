1. Retirement Risk Was Shifted to Workers

For decades, many workers were promised retirement through employer pensions. Over time: pensions declined, individual retirement accounts replaced them, and workers took on market risk. After major market crashes in 2000 and 2008: retirement accounts lost trillions, employers froze or closed pension plans, and those plans were rarely restored. This shift remained in place.

Proof:

  • GAO report on pension freezes and plan terminations
  • SSA analysis of disappearing pensions
  • CRS summary of continued decline

2. The Cost of Living Was Measured Lower Than What People Pay

The government uses official numbers to track how fast prices rise. These numbers are supposed to reflect the “cost of living.”

But the way these numbers are calculated:

  • does not match what people actually pay for housing,
  • uses indirect and delayed methods,
  • and falls behind real rent and home price increases.

Even though this gap is documented, the same numbers are still used to decide:

  • wage increases,
  • benefit adjustments,
  • tax brackets,
  • and major policy decisions.

On paper, inflation looks controlled. In real life, people feel squeezed.

This measurement method stayed in place.

Proof:

  • Official inflation methodology explaining how housing is measured
  • Acknowledgement that housing costs are understated
  • Evidence showing housing prices rising faster than wages

3. Housing Rules Turned High Prices Into Long-Term Debt

There is a federal agency that oversees home loans and the housing finance system.

This agency:

  • sets rules for mortgages,
  • controls how easy or hard it is to get a loan,
  • and influences how much people can borrow.

These rules rely on the same price assumptions described above.

As home prices rose faster than wages:

  • loans became larger,
  • monthly payments increased,
  • and people were locked into decades of debt.

Renters faced higher rents. Buyers faced higher barriers.

These rules stayed in place even as housing became less affordable.

Proof:

  • Federal housing regulator mandate and role
  • Rising mortgage costs and tighter access

4. Warnings Were Given — Decisions Went Ahead Anyway

Before major economic crises, government researchers warned that:

  • asset prices were rising too fast,
  • financial risks were building,
  • and families would be harmed if policies stayed the same.

These warnings were written down and discussed internally.

Even so, the same policy path continued.

After each crisis:

  • markets recovered faster than wages,
  • workers absorbed losses,
  • and the structure did not change.

Proof:

  • Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission — crisis was avoidable and warnings were not acted on
  • Federal Reserve meeting transcript (January 2007)

5. Debt & Scarcity Framing

Government money is created through administrative processes, while taxes are framed as necessary to “pay for” spending and control demand.

This framing enforces long-term financial pressure on households.

Proof:

  • Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis — Open Market Operations
  • Joint Economic Committee — Tax Policy for Economic Growth and Stability

6. None of This Was Reversed

After each major crisis:

  • the problems were studied,
  • reports were written,
  • and harms were acknowledged.

But the core rules stayed the same.

Retirement risk stayed with workers.
Housing stayed unaffordable.
Debt burdens stayed high.
Asset prices recovered faster than wages.

The same structures continued decade after decade.

This pattern repeated.

Proof:

  • Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) — Final Report
  • GAO-16-408 — Retirement Security: Market-Based Model Continued
